I am currently a PhD student in Psychology at Columbia University, where I work with Professor Jon Freeman in the Social Cognitive & Neural Sciences Lab.
Broadly, I am interested in how we form impressions of other people — and, more importantly, why the exact same person can sometimes look completely different depending on what we already know, what we have just seen, or the social environment we happen to be in.
These days, I am especially interested in person perception as it unfolds over time. For example, if you learn something about someone before watching them speak, does that prior knowledge change your impression from the very first seconds of the interaction? Or does it slowly begin to shape what you see as their behavior unfolds?
Basically: how much of the person we think we are seeing comes from the person, and how much comes from everything surrounding them?
Before coming to Columbia, I completed my Master’s degree in Brain and Cognitive Engineering at Korea University under Professor Christian Wallraven, supported by the Global Korea Scholarship (GKS).
During my time living in Korea, I often heard people say: “We can tell 100% whether someone is Korean, Japanese, or Chinese just by looking at their face.” Naturally, I had to ask myself: Really?
In my research, I explored how accurately Koreans could make this fine distinction, which visual cues they relied on, and whether the secret was in external features like hair and face shape or internal ones like the eyes and nose. I even compared human performance with deep learning models and, spoiler alert, the machines won — although they seemed to use very different facial information to do it.
But it didn’t stop there. I also investigated how social impressions such as attractiveness and trustworthiness interact with these judgments. For example, when Korean participants found a Korean face more attractive, they were more likely to categorize it correctly. For Chinese faces, however, the relationship went in the opposite direction.
Note: I still can’t reliably make these distinctions myself. [Try it out and let me know how you do!](https://alllooksame.com/) 🤷‍♀️
I also have a thing for tracking eyes 👀 Using eye-tracking and pupillometry, I have studied how gaze direction, face masks, and emotional expressions influence emotion recognition, and how our eyes navigate faces to pick up subtle social information.
